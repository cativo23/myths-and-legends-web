# Visual brief: Myths and Legends (Salvadoran catalog)

Conventions. **[S]** = sourced: the page was fetched and read. **[S-snippet]** = a search-result summary only; the page itself was not read (some returned 403 or were not opened). **[I]** = my inference or design judgment, not a source claim. All contrast ratios were computed with the WCAG 2.x relative-luminance formula (script run locally), not estimated.

## (a) Key findings

1. The Cipitío is described as a ten-year-old boy with a big belly, oversized conical hat, white cotton clothes and leather sandals. His feet point backward and he bathes in and eats ash. Possible Xipe Totec link in some versions. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cipitio
2. The Siguanaba is a shapeshifter: a beautiful woman with long hair, face hidden by hair or veil, whose true face is a horse head or skull. She appears at night near rivers, streams and lakes. In El Salvador she is cursed Sihuehuet searching for her son Cipitío. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siguanaba
3. The Cadejo is a dog-like spirit of isolated night roads. Eyes are blue when calm and red when hostile. White is benevolent, black malevolent. Salvadoran/Guatemalan versions protect drunk travelers and carry a goat-like smell. Origin is tied to Maya and Nahua animal-companion beliefs. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cadejo
4. Izalco volcano ("El Faro del Pacífico") formed in 1770, erupted almost continuously until 1958, last flank eruption 1966, now quiescent. Height is given as 1,950 m. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Izalco_(volcano) . Britannica reportedly says sailors steered by its glow [S-snippet] https://www.britannica.com/place/Izalco-Volcano (403 on fetch).
5. Fernando Llort's "Estilo Palmeño" (La Palma) is the best-documented Salvadoran folk visual idiom: naive, saturated colors, dark outlines, simple geometric shapes, motifs of houses/hills, doves, suns, churches, mosaic-like structure, with the stated aim of strengthening Salvadoran identity. Treated as a national artist, so copying it reads as imitation; reference the principle (outline, flat color, tessellated community), not the motifs. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernando_Llort
6. Ilobasco miniatures (clay figures 2 to 30 mm; rural, religious, humorous scenes) were declared a Bien Cultural by the Ministry of Culture (Diario Oficial, 18 July 2025). Tools include carved wood palettes and dental-plaster molds. The "sorpresa" is an egg-shaped case hiding a miniature scene. [S] https://www.elsalvador.com/entretenimiento/cultura/miniaturas-de-ilobasco-bien-cultural-el-salvador-ministerio-cultura-patrimonio-artesanos-tradicion-cabanas-diario-oficial/1233480/2025/ (the sorpresa description is [S-snippet] from search results).
7. Ataco murals: PigmenTrip Muralism Festival, 8th edition, inside the Festival del Jaguar. More than 200 murals since it began; imagery includes jaguars, faces, flowers, birds, a girl in a feathered headdress; artists from Colombia, Mexico, Brazil, Peru, Argentina and El Salvador. The article does not describe palette or style. [S] https://kuwaittimes.com/article/48272/lifestyle/art-fashion/winning-back-walls-from-gangs-artists-fill-el-salvadors-towns-with-color/
8. The Festival del Jaguar draws on the "Cabezas del Jaguar", pre-Columbian sculptures in Ataco. The jaguar is said to symbolize strength, power, spirituality and protection. The article does not say what material they are or that the murals depict them. [S] https://www.elsalvador.com/turismo/cultura-viva/estival-jaguar-ataco-ruta-flores/1286479/2026/
9. Indigo (añil): El Salvador exported indigo from about 1760, cultivation was revived from the late 1990s, and it is used to dye traditional garments and early ikat "jaspe" textiles (Guatemala-focused article). Maya blue pigments from indigo appear in pottery and murals. [S] https://revista.drclas.harvard.edu/indigo-in-guatemala/page/2 . Salvadoran indigo was shown at UNESCO in Paris in 2025, and Izalco is listed among indigo municipalities [S-snippet] https://elsalvadorinenglish.com/2025/06/12/el-salvadors-indigo-dye-legacy-draws-global-attention-in-france/ and https://www.elsalvador.com/?p=1242376
10. Hand-painted shop signs (rótulos) with colorful lettering and witty phrases have been part of San Salvador's Centro Histórico for decades and are disappearing. Vintexture (Sofía Ramos, Inés Pacas) archives them as reference for artists and designers, also in Santa Ana, Ahuachapán, Sonsonate. No colors or letter forms are named. [S] https://www.elsalvador.com/?p=1242246
11. A Salvadoran studio, Los Rebusca Estudio (since 2020, 17 characters), already sells a "Lotería de Mitos y Leyendas", a "Lotería Cuscatleca" and folklore comics and books. No visual style is described anywhere I could read. A lotería direction therefore overlaps with an existing local product. [S] https://diario.elmundo.sv/escena/los-rebusca-estudio-lleva-la-cultura-salvadorena-a-libros-comics-y-juegos-de-mesa
12. Web precedents with a clear lesson: Google's Lotería Doodle (five Mexican and Mexican-American illustrators reimagined the cards, multiplayer, a card "caller") and Crafted in Mexico (artisan portraits plus short stories, organized by region) [S] https://blog.google/company-news/inside-google/doodles/loteria/ and https://blog.google/company-news/outreach-and-initiatives/arts-culture/crafted-in-mexico-expands/
13. Nahuizalco night market: lighting moved from candles to electricity; the town is known for mimbre, tule and bejuco (cane/reed/vine) crafts. [S-snippet] https://www.elsalvador.com/turismo/rutas-y-aventuras/nahuizalco-mercado-nocturno-ambiente-familiar/1251702/2025/
14. Lake Ilopango fills a caldera (collapse dated about 431 CE; the 1879-80 eruption raised the Islas Quemadas). [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Ilopango . Olomega has a siren legend near Olomegón island [S-snippet] https://www.elsalvador.com/?p=1249040

## (b) Cultural do / don't

**Do**
- Draw from process and material, not from sacred iconography: indigo dye-bath gradients, resist-dyed (jaspe/ikat) feathering, hand-painted sign lettering, clay-miniature scale, the lotería card as a format. Reason: these are everyday craft vocabularies, tied to living practice and to recognized heritage (Ilobasco is an official Bien Cultural). [S/I]
- Take colors and shapes from the legends' own described attributes: Cadejo blue/red eyes, Cipitío's white cotton and conical hat, Siguanaba's hair-veil, river at night, the Izalco glow. Reason: they are in the stories, so they carry meaning without borrowing someone else's emblems. [S: findings 1 to 4]
- Credit sources and, when real art is added later, credit and pay Salvadoran illustrators and muralists. Reason: the local scene is active (Ataco, Los Rebusca, Vintexture) and the lesson from heritage-design commentary is consultation and consent for commercial use. [S for the scene; S-snippet for ethics, https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/download/5918/4782/7595 and https://www.ecija.com/en/news-and-insights/proteccion-cultural-por-diseno-respuestas-ante-el-extractivismo-digital-de-la-cultura-originaria-en-mexico/]
- Keep the pre-Hispanic layer abstract and generic (geometric step/zigzag rhythm, jaguar as a silhouette idea at most). [I]

**Don't**
- Don't copy glyphs, codex pages, Cabezas del Jaguar, or deity images (e.g., Xipe Totec, linked to Cipitío in some versions). Reason: ceremonial or sacred objects; the rule of thumb in a design-ethics piece is "if you do not know what a symbol communicates, don't use it". [S for the Xipe link: finding 1; S-snippet for the rule: https://www.commarts.com/columns/decolonizing-native-american-design]
- Don't imitate Llort's motifs (doves, suns, churches, all-seeing eye) or Ataco's named murals. Reason: signature of a named national artist and of living muralists. [I, from finding 5]
- Don't caricature: no sombrero-and-mustache folklore tropes, no "jungle tribal" pastiche, no neon "spooky" treatment of the Siguanaba as a pure sexualized monster. The sources frame her as a moral warning and as a cursed mother. [S: finding 2; the caricature advice is [I]]
- Don't use AI-generated entity images as reference or content. The El Diario de Hoy Cipitío/Siguanaba illustrations were flagged by the outlet itself as AI-generated. [S-snippet] https://www.eldiariodehoy.com/?p=96554 (listed in search; the note about AI came from the search summary)
- Don't make the Mexican lotería or Día de Muertos look the default "Latin" skin. Cempasúchil/calavera aesthetics belong to Mexico. [I]
- Don't assume Maya-Lenca visuals are documented here: I found no Lenca motif source for El Salvador, so do not invent any. [S-snippet: Lenca results were Honduran only]

## (c) Three directions

Shared constraints for all three: CSS and inline SVG only, no raster images. Respect `prefers-reduced-motion` (replace motion with fades or static states). Every text/background pair below meets AA (4.5:1 body, 3:1 large text and UI). Palette hex values are my design proposals [I]; the cultural hooks are cited where sourced.

### Direction 1: "Añil" (indigo dye-bath)

**Concept.** The catalog is a length of indigo-dyed cotton: dark blue cloth, undyed cotton paper, and entities "developed" out of the dye like a resist pattern.

**Wow.** Entities materialize the way indigo does: a green-yellow wash that oxidizes to blue (the oxidation behaviour of indigo vat dye is general knowledge, not from a fetched source [I]). Cultural hook: añil is a documented Salvadoran export, revived craft, and traditional jaspe dye [S: finding 9].
- Hero: a full-bleed indigo field with an SVG `feTurbulence` + `feDisplacementMap` feathered edge (the ikat/jaspe "blur" of resist-dyed threads), a huge Fraunces title with the `SOFT` and `WONK` axes tuned, and a slowly drifting low-contrast stripe pattern.
- Cards: "cloth swatches": a rectangle with feathered, hand-dyed edges, thread-count lines (repeating-linear-gradient), a small marigold stitch as category marker (MYTH, LEGEND, TRADITION get three stitch shapes). Kind icons are simple SVG, not literal glyphs.
- Entity detail: on load the header cloth goes from verdigris to indigo (one 1.4 s CSS transition on a gradient and filter); sections are separated by a woven-band divider (SVG pattern). Location chips are "dye stamps".
- Theme: dark = indigo night; light = undyed cotton (Cipitío's white cotton, S finding 1) with indigo ink. Same pattern, inverted.
- Motion: slow oxidation reveals, hover lifts a swatch corner; all off under reduced motion.

**Trade-off and risk.** Most distinctive and most original, and clearly rooted in a recognized Salvadoran craft. Risk: feTurbulence filters are costly on low-end phones (needs a static fallback and a size cap); indigo can read as "fashion brand" rather than "legend" if the entity silhouettes are weak; jaspe is documented in the Guatemala-focused source and I did not confirm its use in Izalco or Nahuizalco, so do not attribute the pattern to a town.

### Direction 2: "Baraja" (popular-print card deck)

**Concept.** Every entity is a numbered card in a hand-printed deck: flat color, thick outline, a caption, a verse.

**Wow.** The home page is a 4-column board of cards that "shuffle" on load and flip on tap; the entity detail is the card enlarged with its "canta" line (a one-line rhyme or description in the voice of a caller). Cultural hook: Salvadoran popular print exists (Lotería Cuscatleca) [S: finding 11]; the card format as a vehicle for cultural symbols is documented [S-snippet] https://peoplesgdarchive.org/item/14998/mexican-loteria-cardsandnbsp . Hand-painted rótulo energy supplies the headline lettering [S: finding 10].
- Hero: a fan of five cards (Cadejo Blanco, Cipitío, Siguanaba, Carreta Chillona, Volcán Izalco) built from CSS transforms, with a slab-type title.
- Cards: paper color, ink outline, offset print shadow, number and name in a banner, category as a colored corner, kind as a small SVG pip.
- Entity detail: the big card at left, the story at right; "Buenas" flip animation on navigation.
- Theme: light = warm paper; dark = near-black table with cards that stay paper-colored, so the print look survives.
- Motion: shuffle, flip, 3D tilt on hover; replaced by fades under reduced motion.

### Direction 3: "Faro" (volcano beacon, tropical night)

**Concept.** The catalog is a night landscape lit by one beacon (the Izalco glow, once a sailors' landmark) and a handful of lamps (candil, fireflies, Cadejo eyes) that mark each entity.

**Wow.** The whole site is a nocturnal map: a layered SVG ridge with the volcano silhouette and a pulsing ember at its summit. Entities appear as lights on the map; hover or focus lights a lamp and lifts its title out of the dark. On the entity detail, the Cadejo eyes in blue (calm) or red (hostile) literally set the page's accent color for that entity [S: finding 3, I for the UI mapping]. Cultural hook: Izalco's "lighthouse" identity [S: finding 4]; lamp-lit night markets in Nahuizalco [S-snippet: finding 13]; Siguanaba's lakes and rivers at night [S: finding 2]; Ilopango and Olomega as teal water [S/S-snippet: finding 14].
- Hero: parallax of 3 SVG ridges (indigo to black), the beacon pulses every 4 s with a conic-gradient light sweep.
- Cards: dark "lantern windows": a small glowing dot per card, a department label, soft inner glow in the category color.
- Entity detail: a dark page with one pool of light around the title that follows the pointer (radial-gradient on a CSS variable); sections reveal as the light passes.
- Theme: dark = night (default); light = "alba", ash-grey dawn with the same ridge in cool greys and the beacon turned into a small ember dot. Dark is the hero experience.
- Motion: beacon pulse, parallax, light-follow; static glow under reduced motion and no pointer tracking on touch.

**Trade-off and risk.** The most atmospheric and the one that best fits "myths of the night". It needs the least illustration skill, since light and type do the work. Risks: the dark theme dominates and the light theme will feel secondary; "dark mystical" is close to what Carlos rejected in round one ("editorial/dark/neutral"), so the saturated ember and teal and the beacon interaction must be strong enough to feel different; the Izalco glow story is a 19th-century and mid-20th-century image (activity ended 1958/1966 [S]), so the site should present it as historical, not current.

## (d) Recommendation (my opinion)

Direction 1, "Añil", with one borrowed move from Direction 3: the pointer-light on the entity detail. Reasons [I]:
- It is the only one whose central idea is a Salvadoran craft with documented heritage and a living revival (añil), rather than a format shared with Mexico (lotería) or a mood close to the rejected dark round (night).
- It works in both themes without one being a leftover (indigo night and undyed cotton are both authentic).
- It needs no illustrator to look finished; the feathered-edge technique carries the identity.
- Direction 2 has the quickest "wow" but a direct local overlap (Los Rebusca) and a Mexican format; Direction 3 is strongest atmosphere but nearest to the rejected direction.
If Carlos wants maximum playfulness over originality, Direction 2 is the pick, but only with a clear Salvadoran differentiator.

## (e) Not verified

- Britannica's Izalco "sailors steered by its glow" claim: only seen in a search snippet; the page returned 403. Wikipedia confirms the nickname, not the navigation use.
- Whether Izalco or Nahuizalco have a distinct textile or weaving tradition: searches found nothing specific (Nahuizalco is documented only for mimbre/tule/bejuco crafts in a snippet; the backstrap-loom heritage found was Panchimalco). I did not confirm that añil is used in Izalco textiles beyond a listing of municipalities.
- Ataco mural style, palette, authorship of the first murals, and any link to the Cabezas del Jaguar: not found. The Cabezas' material and meaning: not found.
- Any palette or typeface actually used in Salvadoran popular design (rótulos, bus paint, Vintexture): not found. The rótulo article gives no colors. The palettes here are my design proposals, not extracted from Salvadoran sources.
- The visual style of Los Rebusca's cards, and any Salvadoran illustrator who has illustrated Cadejo, Siguanaba or Cipitío as a body of work: not found (a UES thesis on digital illustration of legends in Guadalupe, San Vicente exists but I did not read it). Nothing in this brief is an observed Salvadoran illustration style.
- Maya-Lenca and Pipil motifs: no motif vocabulary found. Only general Pipil context (Wikipedia, Getty/Concultura snippets) and a Xipe Totec ceramic in the Museo Nacional de Antropología (snippet). Hence the brief recommends abstraction only.
- Ceramics in Ilobasco: sources disagree on origin (pre-Columbian versus mid-19th-century figure-making); colors of the miniatures were not found.
- Colors of volcano, lakes and rural towns as actually seen: no photographic or art source; the palette mapping is my inference.
- The Olomega siren and Ilopango offering/Snake Woman stories: single-source or unsourced blog; the siren story is a news snippet I did not read; the Ilopango one should not be used.
- Cultural-ethics sources (CUNY huipil, Mexico's 2022 heritage law via a law-firm page, Communication Arts, a University of Cincinnati paper): snippet-level only and none is Salvadoran or about Pipil/Lenca specifically; there is no Salvadoran law or institutional guideline in my findings. Carlos should consult Salvadoran cultural bodies (the Ministry of Culture) before using any real pre-Hispanic motif.
- Web precedents: I found no striking Latin American folklore catalog site with a documented design write-up. Real precedents found: Google Lotería Doodle and Crafted in Mexico (read), PeruDigital and Digital Portobelo (snippets, academic, no design claims). I could not verify any award-winning example; Awwwards searches turned up nothing relevant.
- Typeface glyph rendering of ñ, í, ó in the six chosen families: subsets checked (latin-ext present), actual glyphs not rendered or tested.
- Performance of feTurbulence/displacement on low-end devices: general knowledge, not tested.
- The Atiquizaya lotería (a Salvadoran lotería item appeared as a search-result title, https://www.elsalvador.com/?p=1293681): not read.
